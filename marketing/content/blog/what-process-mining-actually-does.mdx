Every company runs on processes: purchase orders, invoices, tickets, orders, approvals, exceptions, handoffs.

Most companies can describe the intended version. There is an SOP on a wiki page, a Visio diagram in a migration deck, or a manager who knows "how it usually works."

But the actual version is different. It has workarounds, rework, silent waiting, informal approvals, duplicate touches, and that one invoice that sits in someone's inbox for 11 days because the system says it is assigned but nobody feels accountable.

Process mining makes that version visible.

## What it actually is

It takes event logs from your existing systems — ERP, CRM, ticketing — and reconstructs what actually happened. Not what people say happened in interviews. Not what the flowchart on the wall says. What the data says.

Every time someone creates a PO, approves an invoice, escalates a ticket, your system logs that event. Timestamp, case ID, activity name, user. Process mining takes those logs — millions of events, thousands of cases — and builds a process map from data.

The output shows you every path every transaction took. Every variant. Every loop. Every deviation.

Here's the concrete difference.

Your dashboard tells you on-time payment is 91%. Sounds fine. Process mining tells you *why* the other 9% are late — 42% of invoices get touched five or more times before payment, the three-way match fails on 23% of POs because of a unit-of-measure mismatch, and three specific suppliers account for 60% of all late payments.

Your BPM tool models the ideal three-step happy path. Process mining shows you the reality: 14 variants, with the "happy path" accounting for only 35% of cases. The other 65% go through manual overrides, escalations, resubmissions that nobody designed but everyone uses.

An auditor checks a sample once a quarter. Process mining checks every transaction continuously. The auditor might catch that 2% of POs bypass approval. Process mining shows that 2% is $14M in annual spend, concentrated in one business unit, and it started after a reorg six months ago.

RPA automates steps without understanding the process around them. Process mining tells you which steps are worth automating — and after you automate, whether it actually reduced cycle time or just moved the bottleneck somewhere else.

## Where the money hides

This is where it gets interesting. Every process has waste. Not because people are lazy — because complex processes with many handoffs develop inefficiencies that are invisible at the individual transaction level. You only see them in aggregate.

The specific levers, with real numbers:

**Cash discount capture.** Most companies negotiate early payment discounts — 2% off if you pay within 10 days. But invoices get stuck in approval queues and the discount window closes. Deutsche Telekom was capturing 60% of available discounts. After process mining found the specific bottlenecks, they got to 96%. On EUR 7 billion in spend, that's EUR 40 million per year.

**Duplicate payments.** Same invoice entered twice, credit memo doesn't match. Deutsche Telekom found EUR 3 million. An industrial manufacturer recovered $620K in a single quarter.

**Rework and cycle time.** PO rejected and resubmitted. Invoice bounces between AP and the business unit three times. Tech Data reduced P2P cycle time by 57% in one year.

**Maverick spend.** Purchases outside the approved process — no PO, no contract. A medical device company found over $1 million in three months.

**Working capital.** IQVIA increased days payable by 32 days, freeing $600K immediately. A French automotive company found EUR 33 million.

These aren't theoretical. Leading process mining vendors have documented billions in realized value. GE Healthcare freed $1.3 billion in cash flow in a single year. Forrester found a $20 billion company generating $44 million in benefits over three years — 383% ROI, payback under six months.

The numbers are large because the levers compound across every transaction. A 2% improvement in cash discount capture on $500M in spend is millions. A 20-day DSO reduction on $1B in receivables, at 10% cost of capital, frees $5.5M.

Those last two numbers are the ones that matter. Three quarters of enterprises haven't tried it. And of the ones that did, two thirds got disappointing results. Why?

**Data prep eats the project.** 60-80% of a process mining project is data preparation — extracting logs, cleaning timestamps, mapping activities, resolving case IDs. If a project takes six months, four months are getting the data ready. The actual analysis is a fraction.

**Nobody knows how to use it.** PwC found 52% of companies cite lack of tooling or expertise. You need process experts, source system experts, data engineers, analytics developers — all on one team. That's rare.

**Management isn't buying in.** Deloitte 2025 found management buy-in is the #1 barrier at 41%, up from 26% in 2021. Front-line people see the value. Executives view it as "useful but not critical." The bottleneck flipped from awareness to commitment.

**The cost structure doesn't work.** Enterprise platforms cost $1.5-5M in year one — license, implementation, the 15-20 person internal team. That math works for a $20B company finding $44M. It doesn't work for a $500M company where total addressable value is $3M.

## What's changing

Three things converging:

**AI.** 25% currently use AI with process mining, 74% plan to. LLMs can translate natural language into process queries, explain findings automatically, remove the specialized analyst requirement. "Show me why invoice cycle time increased last quarter" becomes a prompt, not a PQL query.

**Platform consolidation.** SAP bought Signavio. Microsoft bought Minit. IBM bought myInvenio. UiPath bought ProcessGold. This isn't a niche category — it's becoming part of the core enterprise stack.

**The mid-market gap.** All leaders are enterprise-only, $1B+ revenue targets. G2 and PeerSpot reviewers keep saying the same thing: pricing needs to come down, complexity needs to come down, time to value needs to be faster. No strong mid-market solution exists yet.

## Does this apply to you?

You don't need to buy anything to think about this. Just ask yourself:

Do you know your *actual* process, or just your intended one? If you drew P2P on a whiteboard, would it match what happens in your ERP?

Are your KPIs healthy but something still feels slow? On-time delivery at 92% sounds good. But if cycle time has 4x variance across product lines, there's waste in the tail.

Do you know where manual work happens? Most companies automate the easy 60%. The other 40% — the exceptions, the "just email it to Sarah" steps — are invisible.

Have you tried to improve a process and it didn't stick? Without seeing the root cause, most improvement projects fix symptoms instead of causes.

If three of those resonate, you have a process visibility problem. The question is not whether process mining would find savings - it will. The real question is whether the available tools match your budget, team, and timeline well enough that you can act on what they find.
