RPA has a success problem. By most analyst estimates, 30-50% of RPA projects fail outright, and another 20-30% deliver less than half their projected ROI. The technology works fine — bots click buttons, fill forms, and move data between systems exactly as instructed. The failure isn't in execution. It's in selection.

Companies automate the wrong things because they don't know what the right things are.

## What RPA actually does

RPA is an execution layer. A bot logs into System A, copies a value, pastes it into System B, clicks submit. It does this faster, cheaper, and more reliably than a human. That's the entire value proposition.

What RPA does not do:

- Decide what should be automated
- Identify where the bottlenecks are
- Measure whether the automation actually helped
- Detect when process behavior shifts and the bot becomes irrelevant

RPA is a power tool. Useful when pointed at the right problem. Destructive when pointed at the wrong one.

```
  What RPA Does vs. What You Need
  ==========================================

  NEED:  "Which of our 400 process variants should we automate?"
  RPA:   "Tell me which buttons to click."

  NEED:  "Where are the 12-day delays hiding in our P2P process?"
  RPA:   "Tell me which fields to fill."

  NEED:  "Did automation actually reduce cycle time?"
  RPA:   "I clicked the buttons you told me to click."
```

RPA executes. It doesn't think. The thinking has to come from somewhere else.

## The 5-stage value chain

Process improvement isn't a single step. It's a pipeline with five distinct stages, each requiring different capabilities.

```
  Process Improvement Value Chain
  ==========================================

  Stage 1: DISCOVER    "What does the process actually look like?"
  Stage 2: DIAGNOSE    "Where are the bottlenecks and deviations?"
  Stage 3: ANALYZE     "What's causing them and what's the impact?"
  Stage 4: AUTOMATE    "Execute the fix — bots, rules, redesigns."
  Stage 5: MONITOR     "Did it work? Is it still working?"
```

Now here's the question: which tools cover which stages?

```
  Tool Coverage Across the Value Chain
  ==========================================

  Stage          Process Mining    RPA    Consultants
  -----------    --------------    ---    -----------
  1. Discover         X                      X
  2. Diagnose         X                      X

  4. Automate                       X
  5. Monitor          X

  Process mining covers 4 of 5 stages.
  RPA covers exactly 1.
```

Consultants can cover the first three stages, but at high cost and low repeatability. They interview stakeholders, draw swim-lane diagrams, and produce a report. Six months later, the process has drifted and the report is stale. Process mining does the same work from event data — continuously, at a fraction of the cost.

RPA without process mining means you're outsourcing stages 1-3 to guesswork, consultant interviews, or the loudest person in the room. Then you build a bot. Then you hope.

## The guesswork approach

Here's how RPA projects typically start without process mining:

1. A consultant or internal team interviews 8-12 stakeholders
2. They compile a "process map" based on what people say happens
3. They identify automation candidates based on perceived pain points
4. They estimate ROI based on self-reported time-per-task
5. They build the bot

The problem at every step: people are bad at describing their own processes. They describe the happy path. They underestimate rework. They overestimate time spent on tasks that feel painful (but take 5 minutes) and underestimate time spent on tasks that feel routine (but take 2 hours because of waiting).


This is why 30% of RPA projects fail. Not because the bots don't work. Because they automate 8-minute tasks while 3-day bottlenecks go untouched.

## Concrete example: invoice processing

Consider a real accounts payable process. A company runs 50,000 invoices per year through their ERP. They're considering RPA to "speed up invoice processing."

Without process mining, the team interviews AP clerks, who report that manual data entry and vendor follow-ups consume most of their time. The RPA vendor builds a bot that automates invoice data entry from PDF to ERP. Cost: $180K. Projected savings: 4 minutes per invoice, or $150K/year.

## The monitoring gap

Here's the part most RPA implementations miss entirely: verification.

You deployed the bot. It's running. Is it actually helping?

RPA tools can tell you the bot ran 11,500 times last month. They can't tell you whether the end-to-end cycle time actually decreased, whether the bottleneck shifted somewhere else, or whether a new process variant emerged that the bot doesn't handle.

Process mining closes this loop. By continuously mining the event log after automation, you get objective measurement:

You'd just celebrate the bot deployment and move on.

## The bottom line

RPA is a hammer. Process mining tells you where the nails are.

Using RPA without process mining means spending $150K-500K on automation projects where you're guessing what to automate, hoping the ROI materializes, and having no systematic way to verify results. That's why 70% of RPA projects underperform.

The fix isn't better bots. It's better targeting. Discover the real process. Diagnose the actual bottlenecks. Quantify the impact. Then — and only then — automate. And keep mining after deployment to verify and adapt.
