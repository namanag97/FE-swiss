Your ServiceNow dashboard says MTTR is 18 hours. SLA compliance is 87%. There are 342 open incidents.

Those numbers matter, but they do not explain the Monday morning argument. Why do P2 network incidents take three times longer than P2 application incidents? Why do some tickets bounce between three assignment groups before anyone owns them? Why do incidents opened after 5 PM breach SLA at twice the daytime rate?

The data to answer those questions is already in ServiceNow. It is sitting in `sys_audit`. Most teams are just looking at it through the wrong lens.

## ServiceNow stores everything

Every time anything changes on an incident record, ServiceNow writes a row to the `sys_audit` table. State changes, assignment group updates, priority adjustments, reassignments, work notes, SLA clock pauses — all of it, timestamped to the millisecond.

```
  sys_audit sample for INC0074821
  ==========================================

  tablename    fieldname          oldvalue            newvalue            sys_created_on
  ----------   ----------------   -----------------   -----------------  -------------------
  incident     state              New                 In Progress        2026-02-10 08:14:32
  incident     assignment_group   Service Desk        Network Ops        2026-02-10 08:14:32
  incident     assigned_to        [empty]             J.Martinez         2026-02-10 08:15:01
  incident     assignment_group   Network Ops         Database Team      2026-02-10 09:42:18
  incident     assigned_to        J.Martinez          R.Chen             2026-02-10 09:42:18
  incident     assignment_group   Database Team       Network Ops        2026-02-10 14:31:05
  incident     assigned_to        R.Chen              K.Patel            2026-02-10 14:31:05
  incident     state              In Progress         Pending            2026-02-10 16:20:44
  incident     state              Pending             In Progress        2026-02-11 09:05:12
  incident     state              In Progress         Resolved           2026-02-11 15:48:33
  incident     state              Resolved            In Progress        2026-02-12 08:22:10
  incident     state              In Progress         Resolved           2026-02-12 11:40:07
  incident     state              Resolved            Closed             2026-02-15 11:40:07
```

That's 13 audit rows for a single incident. Multiply by 50,000 incidents per year and you're looking at millions of rows of process execution data. It's a complete record of how every incident actually moved through your organization.

## But dashboards only show the ending

ServiceNow's built-in reporting and Performance Analytics operate on the `incident` table — the current state of each record. They can tell you:

- **MTTR**: 18 hours (average across all resolved incidents last month)
- **SLA compliance**: 87% (percentage that met the response and resolution targets)
- **Open count**: 342 (snapshot of what's unresolved right now)
- **By priority**: P1 MTTR is 4.2 hours, P2 is 14.6 hours, P3 is 38 hours

These are useful monitoring metrics. They tell you the score. They don't tell you how the game was played. The INC0074821 example above resolves with an MTTR of roughly 52 hours — but the dashboard can't show you that it bounced between Network Ops and Database Team twice, sat idle for 17 hours overnight, and was reopened once after premature resolution.

That incident doesn't show up as a routing problem, a staffing gap, or a quality issue. It shows up as one data point in an 18-hour average.

## Process mining reconstructs the journey

Process mining takes the `sys_audit` data and transforms it into an event log — one row per activity per incident, ordered by timestamp. The case ID is the incident number. The activity is derived from the field change (state transition, assignment change, escalation). The timestamp comes from `sys_created_on`.

From that event log, process mining algorithms reconstruct the actual paths incidents take through your organization. Not the paths documented in your incident management playbook. The real ones.

Here's what becomes visible.

## Variant analysis: the happy path vs. the swamp

Variant analysis groups incidents by the exact sequence of activities they followed. In a typical IT organization processing 50,000 incidents per year, you'll find 200-400 distinct variants. But the distribution is heavily skewed.

```
  Incident Variant Distribution
  ==========================================

  Variant 1 — Happy Path (45% of incidents)
  Create -> Assign L1 -> Work L1 -> Resolve -> Close
  Median MTTR: 4.1 hours

  Variant 2 — Standard Escalation (18% of incidents)
  Create -> Assign L1 -> Work L1 -> Escalate L2 -> Work L2 -> Resolve -> Close
  Median MTTR: 11.3 hours

  Variant 3 — Ping-Pong (9% of incidents)
  Create -> Assign L1 -> Reassign -> Reassign -> Work -> Resolve -> Close
  Median MTTR: 28.4 hours

  Variant 4 — Reopen Loop (6% of incidents)
  Create -> Assign -> Work -> Resolve -> Reopen -> Work -> Resolve -> Close
  Median MTTR: 48.2 hours

  Variant 5 — Escalation + Ping-Pong (5% of incidents)
  Create -> Assign L1 -> Escalate L2 -> Reassign -> Reassign ->
    Escalate L3 -> Work -> Resolve -> Close
  Median MTTR: 62.7 hours

  ...187 more variants (17% of incidents combined)
```

Your MTTR is 18 hours. But 45% of your incidents resolve in 4 hours. The average is dragged up by the 20% that ping-pong, reopen, or get stuck in escalation chains. Those are the incidents worth investigating — and dashboards make them invisible because they're buried in the aggregate.

## Root cause: wrong routing kills resolution time

Filter the process map by incidents that involve two or more reassignments. Now overlay the category and assignment group data from the incident record. A pattern jumps out immediately.

Network P2 incidents assigned to the Database Team first have a median MTTR of 34 hours. Network P2 incidents routed directly to Network Ops have a median MTTR of 6 hours. The difference is the initial misroute — and it happens on 23% of all network incidents.

Why? The assignment rules in ServiceNow use subcategory to route, and "connectivity" maps to Database Team because someone set it up that way three years ago when the connectivity subcategory was mostly database connection pool issues. The infrastructure has changed. The routing hasn't.

```
  Routing Analysis: Network P2 Incidents
  ==========================================

  Correctly routed (77%)
  Create -> Assign Network Ops -> Work -> Resolve
  Median MTTR: 6.1 hours

  Misrouted (23%)
  Create -> Assign DB Team -> [wait 3-8 hrs] -> Reassign Network Ops -> Work -> Resolve
  Median MTTR: 34.2 hours

  Impact: 23% of network incidents carry 28 hours of unnecessary delay
  Fix: Update assignment rule for "connectivity" subcategory
  Effort: 15 minutes in ServiceNow assignment rule config
```

A 15-minute configuration change that process mining surfaced from audit data. No ServiceNow dashboard would ever show this because it requires analyzing the sequence of assignments, not just the final resolution group.

## Bottleneck: the overnight gap

Overlay timestamps on the L1-to-L2 escalation transitions. Incidents escalated between 5 PM and 8 AM have a median wait of 12.4 hours before L2 picks them up. Incidents escalated during business hours wait 1.8 hours.

The cause is straightforward: the L2 team has no night shift coverage. Incidents escalated at 5:30 PM sit in queue until the next morning. But this isn't visible in the MTTR dashboard because it's blended into the overall average. You'd have to build a custom report that segments by escalation time-of-day, and even then you'd see the correlation without understanding that the specific bottleneck is the L1-to-L2 handoff — not L2 work time itself.

```
  L1 → L2 Escalation Wait Time by Hour of Day
  ==========================================

  08:00 - 12:00    1.4 hours avg wait
  12:00 - 17:00    2.1 hours avg wait
  17:00 - 20:00    8.7 hours avg wait
  20:00 - 00:00   13.1 hours avg wait
  00:00 - 08:00   11.8 hours avg wait

  Cases escalated after 5 PM: 31% of all escalations
  SLA breach rate for after-5 PM escalations: 44%
  SLA breach rate for business-hours escalations: 11%
```

This bottleneck analysis directly informs a staffing decision: adding L2 on-call coverage for the 5 PM to midnight window would impact 31% of all escalations and cut their wait time by 10+ hours.

## Conformance: what's being skipped

Your incident management process requires work notes at every state transition for P1 and P2 incidents. Audit requirements, compliance obligations, knowledge management — there are good reasons for the policy.

Process mining can check conformance by comparing every incident's actual event sequence against the expected model. The result: 25% of P1 incidents and 18% of P2 incidents skip work notes on at least one state transition. The worst offender is the Resolve transition — agents close the ticket without documenting what they did.

That's a compliance risk sitting in plain sight, quantified to exact percentages, broken down by assignment group and shift. No amount of dashboard watching finds this. It requires comparing the actual process to the prescribed process, incident by incident, at scale.
